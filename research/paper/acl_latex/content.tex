\begin{abstract}
Natural-language shell assistants are usually built by generation. We audit a published closed-set
alternative: a BM25 retriever over a fixed Windows command list that shows users a confidence and runs its
choice on Enter. Its confidence is no better than a constant forecaster, and wrong answers
average 86\% confidence (v0.1). Under cross-validation on two benchmark versions (the second adds 59
out-of-scope and ambiguous queries), excluding 25 verbatim-copy controls, isotonic recalibration cuts
the shipped confidence's calibration error by 79\% and 78\% (58\% on a label-corrected v0.2),
outside our test family. On v0.2, a cross-validated threshold on the
shipped score rejects 46 of 50 out-of-scope requests but also 20 of 134
in-scope ones; the fixed rule rejects 17 and none, a hybrid detector 34 and 11. Most
out-of-scope requests are everyday ones (AI-assigned labels), and the 35 added were screened for low scores. On
4,500 unscreened external requests (pre-specified check), the threshold still beats the detector (86\% vs.\ 78\%)
but, at equal cost, loses (65\% vs.\ 77\%). The hybrid's confidence
ranks its own errors better, but its accuracy gain is fragile; both systems sometimes return
destructive commands for benign requests. Two annotators' labels agree ($\kappa=0.98$); other analyses are exploratory.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}

Natural-language shell assistance can lower the barrier to command-line tools, but a wrong command can
destroy data. Research since NL2Bash \citep{lin-etal-2018-nl2bash} has mostly generated commands: neural
translation (the NLC2CMD competition, \citealp{agarwal2021nlc2cmd}), then large language model (LLM)
systems (NL2SH; \citealp{westenfelder2025nl2sh}), most recently trained with reinforcement learning on
static-analysis rewards (BashCoder-R1; \citealp{yu2026bashcoderr1}). Retrieving commands instead of generating
them is an older alternative, tested in NLC2CMD \citep{agarwal2021nlc2cmd} and developed in ShellFusion
\citep{zhang2022shellfusion}. Retrieval over a fixed command list cannot invent a command, and a small lexical retriever runs offline and
deterministically, so queries need not leave the user's machine and every answer is a record from a fixed
list that can be inspected; the price is coverage.

We audit one such system as it is published: \toolname{}\toolnote{}, \toolhost{} that maps a query to one of 279
Windows commands with BM25 \citep{robertson2009bm25}, prints the command with a confidence percentage,
refuses below 30\%, and runs the pre-filled, editable command when the user presses Enter (\S\ref{sec:system}).
It was written and released by \authorrel{} before this study, which changed none of its executable code
and none of the corpus fields it reads. We evaluate the released code with the corpus in its source repository,
which adds 51 records committed shortly after the release; the released corpus makes the tool less accurate and
its confidence less calibrated (\S\ref{sec:system}).
The printed confidence is the only reliability signal a user gets, and our reproduction of the tool's baseline
outputs found it frequently confidently wrong. We therefore ask: \emph{how reliable is the confidence of a
shipped closed-set command retriever, and what do post-hoc recalibration, a tuned rejection threshold, and a
hybrid lexical--dense retriever each contribute?}

\paragraph{Contributions.} (1) An audit of a published tool's confidence: it is no better than a constant
forecaster, wrong answers average 86\% confidence (v0.1), and some wrong answers are destructive commands. (2) An
attribution of what fixes what, under cross-validation on two benchmark versions with verbatim-copy controls
excluded: standard recalibration largely corrects the confidence level, a tuned threshold on the shipped score handles
out-of-scope requests at a cost in refused in-scope queries, and a hybrid retriever (a research prototype with one small
MiniLM encoder, not part of the package) mainly improves how confidence ranks the system's own errors. (3) The two
benchmark versions, the code, and a trace from every reported number to its result file, released with the
camera-ready version. The measures are established ones, applied with care for small samples: tie-aware
risk-coverage areas (AURC, AUGRC), a noise floor and a no-skill reference for calibration, and out-of-scope rules
tuned under one protocol and compared at equal false-rejection counts (\S\ref{sec:methods}, \S\ref{sec:ood}).

\section{Related Work}

\paragraph{Natural language to shell.} NL2Bash \citep{lin-etal-2018-nl2bash} paired English descriptions with
Bash commands. The NLC2CMD competition \citep{agarwal2021nlc2cmd} scored each prediction by utility and flag
overlap, weighted by the confidence the system submitted, so confident wrong answers were penalized; one
entry used TF-IDF retrieval with a logistic-regression model that lowered confidence on likely-wrong
predictions, and came within 12\% of the best system. That metric folds confidence into one accuracy-like score;
it does not separate calibration, the ranking of errors and out-of-scope rejection, which we measure
separately. ShellFusion \citep{zhang2022shellfusion} retrieves
commands from question-answer posts, filters them with manual-page and TLDR descriptions, and ranks them
against the query; DocPrompting \citep{zhou2023docprompting} retrieves documentation (for Bash, manual-page
paragraphs) before generating code; Project
CLAI \citep{agarwal2020clai} treats the command line as an environment for AI agents. NL2SH
\citep{westenfelder2025nl2sh} improves LLM translation with parsing, in-context and in-weight learning, and
constrained decoding, QuoteBench
\citep{li2026quotebench} shows that matched execution scores can hide command-path failures, and an
execution-based evaluation covers generated PowerShell as well as Bash \citep{vo2024execution}. We run no
generative system, so our comparison with generation is conceptual.

\paragraph{Reliability.} We draw on calibration \citep{guo2017calibration,naeini2015ece}, post-hoc
recalibration \citep{zadrozny2002isotonic,platt2000probabilistic,zadrozny2001binning}, selective classification
and its risk-coverage metrics \citep{geifman2017selective,geifman2019aurc,traub2024overcoming}, and out-of-distribution (OOD) detection
from confidence scores \citep{hendrycks2017baseline}. The closest task framing is intent classification with
out-of-scope queries \citep{larson-etal-2019-evaluation}; the closest methods are learned calibrators for
abstention under domain shift \citep{kamath-etal-2020-selective} and confidence estimation for mapping language to
programs \citep{dong-etal-2018-confidence}. In text-to-SQL, selective classifiers mostly catch irrelevant questions
rather than wrong queries \citep{somov2025texttosql}, which our out-of-scope breakdown echoes (\S\ref{sec:ood}).
Within our targeted search (Limitations), natural-language-to-shell retrieval work has not audited the confidence
a released tool shows its users, nor separated what recalibration, a tuned rejection threshold and a hybrid
retriever each fix, with out-of-scope rejection weighed against its false-rejection cost; to our knowledge, that
combination is what is new here, not any single method.

\section{System and Benchmark}
\label{sec:system}

\paragraph{The published tool.} Queries are tokenized (lowercase, all characters except ASCII letters, digits,
spaces and hyphens removed, 16-word stopword list), so non-English queries are refused. Each record's intent,
category and description is scored with BM25 ($k_1=1.2$, $b=0.75$), plus $+15$ when the query, stripped to
letters and digits, is a substring of the equally stripped intent or contains it; this can fire on an accidental
substring (the bare query ``tar'' matches ``start''). Confidence is $\min(\mathrm{round}(s/8\times100),100)$ for
top score $s$, and is 0 when $s<2.0$. The command-line tool prints the top command with ``confidence: $N$\%'',
refuses when $N<30$, and otherwise opens an editable prompt pre-filled with the command; a single Enter runs it
through PowerShell on Windows (the default POSIX shell elsewhere). No risk level is shown. Retrieval is local; an
optional query-sync feature is off by default. Our evaluation counts a query as answered when $s\geq2.0$; no
benchmark query scores between 2.0 and 2.4, so this makes the same decisions as the 30\% rule. We reproduced the
archived baseline outputs exactly (0/150 mismatches). Mean latency of one retrieval call, which rebuilds the index,
was 3.3\,ms in one run on one machine (process start-up excluded), an indicative figure.

\paragraph{Corpus.} 431 records, of which 279 are visible on Windows (145 cross-platform, 134
Windows-specific), each a distinct intent; the tool also indexes one packaged user snippet, so its Windows index
holds 280 records. The records were checked by script rather than one by one, and a few Windows-visible records
are Linux commands (e.g., \texttt{sudo reboot}). The released package has identical code but an earlier corpus of
380 records (240 visible on Windows); the 51 later records were committed shortly after the release and never
published. Rerun with the released corpus, 17 of the 150 v0.1 answers change, accuracy falls from 67.3\% to
58.7\%, and the raw ECE of its confidence rises from 0.323 to 0.372 (v0.2: 0.293 to 0.315); 15 v0.1 items can be
answered only with the later records, so we evaluate the repository corpus.

\paragraph{Benchmark.} v0.1 has 150 hand-adjudicated queries of 9 types, labeled CORRECT (119), AMBIGUOUS
(14), OOD (15; here, out-of-scope requests that no record serves) or NEEDS\_CORRECTION (2), with a risk level for the
gold command. All 25 \emph{canonical} queries repeat a corpus intent verbatim and every system answers them correctly, so
we treat them as positive controls and exclude them from headline figures. v0.2 contains all of v0.1 plus 35
out-of-scope and 24 ambiguous queries, so the versions share their 121 answerable queries and are not
independent. The new queries were drafted and adjudicated by an AI agent under human direction, and every
draft was run through both BM25 and dense retrieval: 24 of 30 ambiguous drafts were kept (6 failed the ambiguity
or novelty criteria), and all 35 out-of-scope drafts were kept unchanged, their top-1 BM25 score and dense
similarity having been checked to be low (maximum 7.39 and 0.31). The 15 v0.1 out-of-scope queries were instead
checked by searching all corpus intents and descriptions for relevant keywords, not against retrieval scores.
Seventeen of the 24 new ambiguous queries are single tool or category names (``grep'', ``pip''); nine of v0.1's
14 are too. Public natural-language-to-Bash benchmarks target Linux commands and do not map onto this Windows
corpus, so we did not use them. Two items have gold commands outside the Windows corpus (\qid{145}, and
\qid{187} in v0.2 only), and one (\qid{149}) has an acceptable command outside it; every system misses the first
two and answers \qid{149} correctly, so they lower every system's non-OOD accuracy by one query on v0.1 and two on
v0.2 and change no comparison. One partially independent reviewer, shown only the query text, re-labeled a
stratified sample of 14 AI-authored queries: Cohen's $\kappa=0.63$ \citep{cohen1960coefficient} (post hoc bootstrap
95\% interval [0.39, 1.00]), below our 0.7 target. The reviewer agreed with all 8 out-of-scope labels (exact 95\%
interval for the agreement rate [63, 100]\%; 7 everyday requests and one far-from-corpus computing task, none a
terminal task) and disagreed on 3 of 6 ambiguous ones. We then wrote a codebook and, before any label existed,
fixed a two-annotator protocol with two criteria ($\kappa\geq0.7$; both annotators keep at least 90\% of out-of-scope
labels). Two annotators, who both confirmed working independently and were not shown the original labels, labeled
58 AI-authored queries (one more, \qid{187}, has the defect above) and 20 controls using the codebook and the
corpus: $\kappa=0.98$ (95\% interval [0.94, 1.00], 78 items), and both kept all 35 out-of-scope labels, so both
criteria were met. Both judged 4 of the 23 ambiguous queries clear and split on one; with no third reader, the
protocol keeps the 4 labels and drops the split item. Benchmark v0.2.1 applies this and corrects \qid{145} and
\qid{149} (Appendix~\ref{app:labels}).

\section{Methods}
\label{sec:methods}

\paragraph{What is compared.} (i) The shipped confidence, before and after post-hoc recalibration. (ii) Three
out-of-scope rules: the shipped fixed rule ($s<2.0$), a threshold on the shipped score $s$ tuned on development
folds, and the hybrid detector. (iii) Three retrievers: BM25, dense retrieval with a 384-dimensional MiniLM
sentence encoder (\texttt{all-MiniLM-L6-v2}; \citealp{wang2020minilm,reimers-gurevych-2019-sentence}), and a hybrid
that min-max normalizes both score lists over each query's candidates and ranks by
$\alpha\,\mathrm{BM25}_{\mathrm{norm}}+(1-\alpha)\,\mathrm{dense}_{\mathrm{norm}}$. The hybrid's confidence is
its fused top-1 score, which equals 1 whenever the two retrievers agree on the top command; its detector rejects
when that score falls below a threshold. Appendix~\ref{app:spec} gives the full specification. None of the
calibrators, tuned thresholds, detectors, the risk classifier or the dense and hybrid retrievers is part of the
published tool; all are offline analyses of its outputs.

\paragraph{Protocol.} Five folds (seed 42, stratified by label; Split A). For each test fold, $\alpha$ (grid
0.0--1.0, maximizing non-OOD accuracy), the detector thresholds (maximizing F1) and the calibrators are chosen
on the other four folds and applied once. Each threshold is chosen from the scores observed on the development
folds, with out-of-scope predicted below it; ties in F1 go to the lowest such score. There is no inner split;
each query's hybrid score uses the $\alpha$ chosen for its own fold ($\alpha\in\{0.3,0.5\}$ throughout). The
features for the hybrid's out-of-scope and ambiguity detectors were chosen by inspecting class means on the full
data, a leak that may flatter the detector's figures; the tuned shipped threshold uses the tool's own score
and needed no such choice. A second split groups queries by intent (Split B),
which checks for tuning leakage across intents but cannot test unseen commands, since the corpus always holds the
gold intent (Appendix~\ref{app:secondary}).

\paragraph{Metrics and statistics.} Expected calibration error (ECE) uses 10 equal-width bins
\citep{naeini2015ece,guo2017calibration}; we also report equal-mass and sweep estimates, a noise floor (the ECE of a
perfectly calibrated forecaster with the same confidences and $n$, simulated), and Brier skill
\citep{brier1950verification} against an out-of-fold no-skill forecaster that predicts the development base rate.
Confidence often ties at its maximum (64\% of the hybrid's and 73\% of the shipped values on all 150 v0.1
queries), so
risk-coverage curves use expected risk under a random order within ties and are summarized by the area under them (AURC; \citealp{geifman2019aurc}) and the area under the
generalized risk-coverage curve (AUGRC; \citealp{traub2024overcoming}); the area under the receiver
operating characteristic curve (AUROC) for separating right from wrong answers by confidence, which we call
correctness AUROC, measures ranking alone. Accuracy differences use the exact McNemar test \citep{mcnemar1947note}, proportions
Wilson intervals \citep{wilson1927probable}, and effects paired percentile bootstraps (10,000 resamples, seed 42),
which are not dual to the exact test. A family of four comparisons per version (accuracy vs.\ BM25 and vs.\ dense,
out-of-scope rejection by the hybrid detector, hybrid ECE reduction) is Holm-corrected \citep{holm1979simple}; it
was fixed after the raw results were seen, so \emph{all significance statements outside the external check are exploratory}. The shipped
confidence's recalibration and the tuned shipped threshold are outside this family.

\paragraph{External check.} One analysis was specified and frozen in a tagged commit before any of its
queries was scored. It applies the out-of-scope rules, unchanged, to the two test sets of CLINC150
\citep{larson-etal-2019-evaluation}, requests written for a general-purpose task assistant: 4,500 from 150
intents and 1,000 outside them. Both sets are treated as out of scope: no corpus record performs any intent's
request (checked per intent before scoring, so no intent was excluded), and the 1,000 are assumed to be. The
check therefore measures rejection only, not refused in-scope queries, and is general-domain rather than
terminal-specific. The tuned thresholds are v0.2's, unchanged; all five per-fold values are applied and the
median rate is reported. The primary comparison is the tuned threshold against the detector; a secondary one
compares the two scores at equal v0.2 cost, each thresholded to refuse at most 11 of v0.2's 159 in-scope
queries. Intervals on the 4,500 resample intents.

\paragraph{The shipped confidence.} The published baseline answers 67.3\% of all 150 v0.1 queries correctly (65.5\%
of the non-control in-scope ones, Table~\ref{tab:acc}), yet its 49 wrong answers, none of them controls, carry a mean confidence of 86\%, and
44.9\% of them are shown at 100\%. Its ECE (0.323 on v0.1,
0.293 on v0.2, controls excluded) is five to six times the noise floor of a perfectly calibrated forecaster at this size
(0.064 and 0.050), and its Brier score is no better than predicting the base rate for every query (skill $-0.23$
and $-0.06$, intervals including zero). A heuristic score such as $s/8$ capped at 100\% is not expected to be
calibrated; what the audit adds is how far off it is, and that the tool shows it to users as a percentage.
Figure~\ref{fig:shipped} shows the gap: 77\% and 63\% of non-control queries fall in the top bin, shown at a mean
99\% confidence, and 70\% and 73\% of them are answered correctly.

\paragraph{Recalibration.} Isotonic regression fit on development folds lowers the shipped confidence's ECE to
0.069 on v0.1 and 0.063 on v0.2, reductions of 79\% and 78\%, within the noise floor's 95th percentile
(0.105 and 0.080) on this fold partition; across 20 partitions the reduction is at least 45\% and 63\%, but the
recalibrated ECE is within that percentile on only 18 and 13 of them (Appendix~\ref{app:secondary}). On the
label-corrected v0.2.1, whose stratified folds differ, the reduction is 58\% (to 0.119, above the 95th percentile
0.085), and the recalibrated ECE is within that percentile on 4 of 20 partitions (Appendix~\ref{app:labels}). Equal-mass bins give 0.110 and 0.070 (noise-floor 95th percentiles 0.122 and 0.092) and the
bin-count sweep 0.060 and 0.047; before recalibration all three estimators give the same 0.323 and 0.293. Brier skill becomes positive (0.20 and 0.33, intervals excluding zero), so the
recalibrated number carries information about correctness.
Platt scaling and histogram binning, fit on the same folds, give ECEs with overlapping bootstrap intervals (v0.1:
0.087 and 0.101; v0.2: 0.045 and 0.039), but we did not test the differences between calibrators; histogram binning's ECE is partly measured on the bins it fits, and
it has the worst Brier score of the three in every case. The hybrid's own confidence behaves similarly: raw, its
Brier skill is $-0.32$ and $-0.30$, both intervals excluding zero; after recalibration its ECE falls from 0.329 to
0.108 and from 0.368 to 0.071, above the noise floor's 95th percentile on v0.1 (0.089) and within it on v0.2
(0.079). Its raw ECE is the same under all three estimators; after recalibration, equal-mass bins give 0.108 and
0.058 and the sweep 0.055 and 0.071. This hybrid reduction is the Holm-family comparison, tested with controls included, and it survives Holm
correction on both versions (Table~\ref{tab:holm}). Recalibration changes the number shown, not the answer or its
ranking: correctness AUROC even drops (shipped, v0.1: 0.742 to 0.686), because each fold gets its own map.
The maps are fit on benchmark queries written by the authors and an AI agent, so they hold for queries like these;
a deployed calibrator would be fit on all labeled queries, and whether its map transfers to real user queries is
untested.

\paragraph{Out-of-scope requests.}
\label{sec:ood}
The shipped fixed rule rejects 17 of v0.2's 50 out-of-scope requests. The hybrid detector rejects 34 (two-sided
exact McNemar against the fixed rule, Holm-adjusted $p=0.00006$), at a cost of 11 false rejections among 134 in-scope queries, three of
which the hybrid had answered correctly. On this benchmark, the gain comes from the threshold, not the hybrid. The shipped score
itself separates out-of-scope requests better than the hybrid's fused score (pooled AUROC 0.956 vs.\ 0.889 on v0.2,
0.939 vs.\ 0.837 on v0.1), and a threshold on the shipped score tuned exactly like the detector rejects 46 of 50
(McNemar $p=0.004$ against the detector) at 20 false rejections ($p=0.093$); on v0.1 it rejects 10 of 15 and the
detector 7 ($p=0.25$), at 9 and 6 false rejections. The two tuned rules thus sit at different operating points.
Compared at equal false-rejection counts, with each score's threshold picked on the same data (an in-sample,
descriptive comparison), the shipped score and the hybrid's feature reject 23 and 21 of 50 with no false
rejections, 33 and 33 at 7, and 43 and 37 at 15 (v0.1: 5 and 1 at none, 10 and 7 at 6); no control is among these
false rejections. On the benchmark, the shipped score is never worse at the points we checked, and the tuned threshold's larger count
comes mostly from accepting more false rejections.

By kind of request (labels assigned by an AI assistant, unchecked), the fixed rule, tuned threshold and detector
reject 14, 34 and 25 of 34 everyday non-computing requests; 3, 4 and 4 of 5 far-from-corpus computing tasks; 0, 1
and 1 of the one nonsensical request; and 0, 7 and 4 of the 10 terminal tasks the corpus does not cover. One caveat
favors the shipped score: the 35 added queries were checked to have low raw BM25 scores (at most 7.39), and the
tuned shipped thresholds (6.50--7.20) sit just below that maximum. The hybrid's thresholds (0.884--0.925) are on the
fused, per-query-normalized scale and cannot be compared with the 7.39 and 0.31 bounds. On the 15 original queries,
which were not checked against scores, the three rules reject 4, 12 and 9 in this v0.2 run (4, 10 and 7 in the v0.1
run, Table~\ref{tab:claims}); on the 35 added ones, 13, 34 and 25. The
threshold's lead over the detector therefore also appears on the unscreened queries, though 15 queries are few. (The
detector's gain over the fixed rule on the same 15 queries has $p=0.25$ in the v0.1 run and $p=0.0625$ in the v0.2
run, because each run tunes its own thresholds; neither is significant, and the second is not a replication.)

On the external check (Appendix~\ref{app:external}), the fixed rule rejects only 25\% of the 4,500 CLINC150
requests from known intents and 19\% of the 1,000 others: those that share no word, after stop-words,
with the corpus, except three short greetings (e.g., ``bye'') accepted through an accidental substring match
(\S\ref{sec:system}). At their v0.2 operating points the tuned threshold rejects 86\% and the detector 78\%
(difference $+8.3$ points, interval [5.0, 11.6]; 83\% and 78\% on the 1,000), a smaller lead than on v0.2. At
equal v0.2 cost, however, the hybrid's feature rejects more than the shipped score (77\% vs.\ 65\%, $-12.5$
points [$-16.2$, $-8.9$]; 77\% vs.\ 60\% on the 1,000). On these requests, then, the benchmark's ordering of
the two scores is reversed. That fits the screening caveat above, but the populations also differ, and the
check measures no false rejections.

\paragraph{What the hybrid adds.} The hybrid's confidence ranks its own errors better than the shipped confidence:
tie-aware AURC and AUGRC are lower on both versions with intervals excluding zero (Table~\ref{tab:claims}), and
correctness AUROC is higher, though on v0.2 without controls its interval just includes zero. At 50\% coverage, which
lies inside the maximum-confidence tie block, the hybrid's expected error on all 150 v0.1 queries is 8.3\% (0--10.7\%
depending on how ties are ordered). Its accuracy gain is consistent in size but not established (Table~\ref{tab:acc}): over BM25 it
gains 6.4 points on v0.1 (exact $p=0.0156$, Holm-adjusted 0.047) and 4.5 on v0.2 ($p=0.070$), resting on 7 and 8
discordant queries, and one bare keyword (``system'') decides the v0.2 test, whose verdict also depends on the test used (mid-p 0.039; Appendix~\ref{app:secondary});
a benchmark this size has little power
for gains of this size \citep{card2020littlepower}. Its advantage over dense retrieval on v0.2 depends on three
bare-keyword queries (Appendix~\ref{app:secondary}). Grouping folds by intent leaves accuracy unchanged and weakens

\paragraph{Risky wrong answers.} The case that motivates the audit does occur (Table~\ref{tab:risk}). Classifying each

\section{Discussion}

For a shipped closed-set assistant the three problems separate cleanly. The displayed confidence was the weakest
part, and standard recalibration on held-out labels repairs most of it on our benchmark (a result outside the
corrected family); on queries like these, the number a user sees can become informative without changing the
retriever. Whether a better-calibrated number changes what users run is untested. Out-of-scope rejection depended
mostly on the threshold: tuned on the shipped score, it rejected more out-of-scope requests by refusing more in-scope
queries, a trade-off that should be set by the relative cost of a wrong command and a refusal. Which score to
threshold is not settled: the shipped score did at least as well as the hybrid's feature at equal false-rejection
counts on our benchmark (in-sample), but worse on the external requests at equal v0.2 cost. The
hybrid's value, with the one encoder we tested, lies in ranking its own errors, which matters for abstention. For
users of a tool that runs a command on Enter, the risky wrong answers suggest safeguards independent of calibration.
The tool runs a command only after a single Enter on a prompt pre-filled with it; beyond that, it could show the risk level and
ask for confirmation (PowerShell's \texttt{-Confirm}) or offer a dry run (\texttt{-WhatIf}) before destructive
commands, or require higher confidence before returning a high-risk command. We did not evaluate any of these.

\section{Conclusion}

On our benchmark, the three reliability problems of a shipped closed-set retriever had three different remedies,
and a better retriever was not needed for out-of-scope requests, although on external requests the hybrid's
feature rejected more at equal cost. A published natural-language command retriever shows its users a confidence that, on our benchmark, is no better
than a constant forecaster. On the same benchmark, post-hoc recalibration fixes most of that. A threshold on the
shipped score, tuned by cross-validation, rejects more out-of-scope requests than a hybrid detector (on v0.2, 46 vs.\
34 of 50, and 12 vs.\ 9 on the 15 not screened by retrieval score) but refuses more in-scope queries (20 vs.\ 11 of 134), and
most of these requests are everyday rather than terminal tasks. In an analysis frozen before scoring, on external
general-domain requests, the threshold still rejects more at its operating point (86\% vs.\ 78\%), but at equal
v0.2 cost the hybrid's feature rejects more (77\% vs.\ 65\%). The hybrid, with one small encoder (MiniLM), mainly improves how
confidence ranks its own errors. Next steps, in order:
release the corrected benchmark (v0.2.1); extend the pre-specified external check,
so far general-domain, to answerable and terminal-task out-of-scope queries judged by people; build an independent
safety set; and study whether calibrated confidence and risk prompts change what users run.

\section*{Limitations}

\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{Small, dependent, partly AI-authored benchmark.} 150 and 209 queries, the second containing the first;
  59 queries drafted by an AI agent. Two annotators agree on their labels ($\kappa=0.98$), but the codebook uses the
  benchmark's own definitions, so agreement is partly by construction; its worked examples were written by an AI
  assistant, and it was written after the first check ($\kappa=0.63$, $n=14$); blinding and independence rest on the
  annotators' own compliance, as the repository is public; and with no third reader, 4 labels both annotators
  questioned were kept. Two items have gold commands outside the corpus and one an acceptable command; v0.2.1
  corrects or drops them, and its re-run weakens the v0.2 calibration result (Appendix~\ref{app:labels}). Queries were written by the authors and an AI agent,
  not collected from users, so calibration and out-of-scope rates depend on this mix. A few accepted commands are
  POSIX-only and would not run in PowerShell; the shipped tool is credited with 3 such answers on v0.1 and 4 on v0.2
  (e.g., \texttt{sudo reboot} for the bare query ``system''), so its accuracy is slightly overstated. The tool, its
  corpus and the benchmark were built by the same people, so queries may follow the corpus's phrasing more closely
  than users' would; we exclude verbatim copies of corpus intents, but milder overlap may remain.
  \item \textbf{Exploratory statistics.} Every analysis except the external check (Appendix~\ref{app:external}) and the two-annotator analysis
  (Appendix~\ref{app:labels}) was specified after results were seen, including the Holm
  family, the controls exclusion, the equal-false-rejection comparison (whose thresholds are chosen in-sample) and
  the sensitivity subsets. Accuracy tests rest on 7--8 discordant queries against BM25 and 12--17 against dense. Headline results use one fold partition
  (seed 42); Appendix~\ref{app:secondary} repeats every fold-dependent step on 20 partitions, as point estimates.
  \item \textbf{Out-of-scope evidence.} Only 10 of the benchmark's out-of-scope queries are terminal tasks outside
  the corpus; the kind labels are AI-assigned; the added queries were checked with raw retrieval scores, which favors
  the shipped score; and the hybrid detector's features were chosen on the full data, which may flatter its
  out-of-scope counts and AUROC, the ambiguity-detection results and A4/A5. The external check bears on the last
  two: on unscreened requests the shipped score lost its advantage at equal cost, and the detector rejected more,
  not less, than on v0.2. It does not bound either bias, since its population differs.
  \item \textbf{External check.} Its requests are general-domain, written for a task assistant, so it does not
  establish how the rules handle out-of-scope terminal tasks, the case that matters most for a command tool. It has
  no in-scope queries, so it measures rejection but not the cost in refused requests; the false-rejection counts
  are v0.2's. The requests outside CLINC150's intents are assumed out of scope, not checked one by one; the
  equal-cost thresholds are set in-sample on v0.2 and refuse 10 and 11 in-scope queries rather than exactly the
  same number; and, like the rest of the study, the check covers one tool and one corpus.
  \item \textbf{Scope.} One tool, evaluated with its repository corpus rather than the smaller released one
  (\S\ref{sec:system}), one platform (Windows; results depend on the platform-filtered corpus), 279 commands,
  one small sentence encoder and one fusion rule, exact-match scoring against gold commands, and single-machine
  latencies. No generative system or user study is evaluated, since our question is the shipped retriever's
  confidence, and public natural-language-to-Bash benchmarks target Linux rather than this Windows corpus.
  \item \textbf{Safety.} The risk classifier is scored against the benchmark's own labels, two of which are
  inconsistent, and misses at least one destructive command; functional evaluation covers 15 queries and treats exit
  code 0 as success.
  \item \textbf{Literature.} Our search was targeted (natural-language-to-shell generation and retrieval, calibration,
  selective prediction, out-of-scope detection), last updated in October 2026; the novelty claim is limited to the
  combination evaluated here.
\end{itemize}

\section*{Ethical Considerations}

The published tool runs the command it retrieves when the user presses Enter, and our results show it can offer a
destructive command for a benign request, sometimes at maximum confidence. We report these cases rather than a safety
claim, and recommend that such tools show a risk level and require explicit confirmation for high-risk commands. This
study does not change the published package, so its findings apply to current users; with the released corpus the
tool is, if anything, less accurate and less calibrated (\S\ref{sec:system}). No command was run on a user's system:
the functional check ran commands in disposable temporary directories on our own machine, not in an OS-level
sandbox. We disclose that part of the benchmark was
written by an AI agent, that our first label check fell below target, and that the annotation codebook's worked
examples were written by an AI assistant. Code, benchmark files, result files and a
reproduction guide will be released with the camera-ready version; they are withheld from the review version for
anonymity.

\section{Two-Annotator Study and Benchmark v0.2.1}
\label{app:labels}

\paragraph{Protocol.} The protocol was committed before any label existed. Two annotators labeled each query clear,
ambiguous or out of scope relative to the corpus, from a written codebook and the corpus, without the original
labels: the 59 AI-authored queries and 20 controls (4 of them verbatim attention checks). \qid{187}, whose gold
command is outside the Windows corpus, is excluded from the primary statistic as a known defect. The primary
statistic is unweighted Cohen's $\kappa$ over the remaining 78 items, with a bootstrap interval. A third reader was
to resolve items on which the annotators disagree (contested) or agree against the original label (reversed); with
none available, the pre-declared fallback drops contested items and keeps reversed items' labels.

\paragraph{Results.} $\kappa=0.980$ [0.937, 1.000] (98.7\% agreement); on the 58 AI-authored queries alone,
$\kappa=0.967$ [0.897, 1.000]. Both annotators kept all 35 out-of-scope labels. Of the 23 ambiguous queries, 18
were confirmed; both annotators judged four clear (``search for a file'', ``list running processes'', ``check
network connection'', ``copy a file''), which are reported, not relabeled; one, ``stop a process'', was contested
and dropped. On the 14 queries the first reviewer also labeled, the two annotators agree on all 14.

\paragraph{Disclosures.} The codebook was written after the first check exposed disagreements, and its worked
examples were written by an AI assistant; it adopts the benchmark's own definitions, so some agreement is by
construction. The repository is public, so blinding to the original labels rests on the annotators' compliance,
and independence on their confirmation to the author. One annotator was asked to re-check a column listing the
corpus records consulted, which enters no statistic; the returned file also changed labels and comments and
closely matched the other annotator's sheet (37 of 79 comments identical to it and 11 more matching its
beginning), so it was not used. The original returns, in which no comment matches the other annotator's, are the
basis of every statistic, and both annotators confirmed they worked independently.

\paragraph{Benchmark v0.2.1.} v0.2.1 has 207 queries: it replaces \qid{145}'s gold command with a corpus record,
removes \qid{149}'s non-corpus acceptable command, and drops \qid{187} and the contested query. \qid{145}'s status
moves from needs-correction to correct; no other label changes.
Every analysis was re-run with unchanged code. Removing two queries redraws the stratified folds (37 of the 207
queries keep theirs), so the differences in Table~\ref{tab:v021} mix the corrections with a new partition. On all 20
partitions of v0.2.1, the hybrid's confidence still ranks its errors better and the tuned threshold still rejects
more out-of-scope requests than the detector. The calibration result weakens: the recalibrated shipped ECE is within
the noise floor's 95th percentile on 4 of 20 partitions (v0.2: 13), with a median reduction of 67\% (v0.2: 78\%).
The AUGRC difference's interval now reaches zero, and the hybrid's accuracy gain over dense no longer survives Holm
correction ($p=0.070$).
